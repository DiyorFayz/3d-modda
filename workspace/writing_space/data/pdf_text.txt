                             Four BCR–ABL inhibitors
                        Read the landmarks. Learn the differences.




                   Imatinib mesylate · Nilotinib · Dasatinib · Bosutinib


1 · Learn the drawing language
Executive Summary
Learn the landmarks before memorizing the whole molecule. These four established
medicines inhibit BCR–ABL, a protein involved in certain leukemias. This guide teaches their
existing structures, not new AI-designed drugs. “Dositinib” is interpreted as dasatinib, as
confirmed. [3–7]

Five rules for reading a skeletal structure
  1. An unlabeled corner or line end is carbon. Hydrogens attached to carbon are usually
     omitted; ordinary neutral carbon normally has four bonds in total.
  2. One, two or three lines mean a single, double or triple bond. A double bond counts
     twice toward carbon’s usual four bonds.
  3. Letters identify other atoms: N = nitrogen, O = oxygen, S = sulfur, F = fluorine and Cl
     = chlorine. Cl is one atom symbol, not C followed by a letter I.
  4. A polygon is a ring, but not every ring is aromatic. Alternating bonds are a common
     way to draw an aromatic ring; its electrons are delocalized. Piperazine is non-aromatic.
  5. A 2D drawing shows connections, not the molecule’s exact 3D pose. Rotating a
     drawing does not change its identity.

2 · Imatinib mesylate
First recognize the imatinib molecule




Figure 2: Imatinib, shown as the neutral parent for learning connectivity. The mesylate
counterion is not part of this drawing. Sources: PubChem CID 5291 and ChEBI:45783. [1,2]


Read it in four chunks
  • A · Pyridine + pyrimidine: find the two aromatic six-membered rings containing nitro-
    gen. Pyridine has one ring N; pyrimidine has two ring Ns. They are connected rings, not
    fused rings.
  • Two benzene rings: the remaining aromatic six-membered rings have only carbon in
    their ring skeletons. Substituents branch from them.
  • B · One amide: locate C(=O)–NH. The carbonyl is C=O; the adjacent nitrogen makes this
    group an amide, rather than a ketone.
  • C · Piperazine: find the non-aromatic six-membered ring with two opposite nitrogens.
    One N carries a methyl group; the other connects through a methylene group, –CH₂–, to a
    benzene ring. [1,2]
Memory cue: “Imatinib = nitrogen-ring pair + amide bridge + piperazine tail.” This is a recog-
nition mnemonic, not a binding model.
What does “mesylate” add?
Mesylate means methanesulfonate, CH₃SO₃⁻. Imatinib mesylate is a salt: a protonated
imatinib ion is accompanied by the negatively charged mesylate counterion. A simplified book-
keeping expression is:

                                                             −
                                 [imatinib + H]+   +   CH3 SO3

This is not a synthesis reaction. There is no covalent bond joining mesylate to imatinib. The
neutral drawing above makes comparison easier; it does not specify the drug’s protonation at
every pH. Gleevec is labeled as imatinib mesylate. [3]
Check yourself: Is the sulfur in “imatinib mesylate” inside the imatinib parent? No—it is in
the counterion.

3 · Nilotinib
Start with the familiar, then spot the differences




Figure 3: Nilotinib, shown as the neutral parent without hydrochloride or water of crystalliza-
tion. Sources: PubChem CID 644241 and ChEBI:52172. [1,2]


Read it in four chunks
  • A · Familiar six-membered N rings: nilotinib also contains a pyridine–pyrimidine
    pair. Use these as your first landmark when comparing it with imatinib.
  • B · An amide, but check its direction: follow the connected atoms, not just the overall
    shape. In imatinib, the benzene on the pyrimidine side connects to the amide N. In nilotinib,
    the corresponding side connects to the carbonyl carbon. The two molecules are not
    identical scaffolds with a tail simply swapped.
  • A · A five-membered imidazole ring: this aromatic ring contains two nitrogens. Do
    not confuse it with six-membered, non-aromatic piperazine.
  • D · A trifluoromethyl group: one carbon carries three fluorine atoms, written CF₃. It
    attaches to a benzene ring. Those fluorines are covalently part of nilotinib, not counterions.
    [1,2]
Memory cue: “Nilotinib = familiar N-ring pair, plus imidazole and three Fs.”
What is absent matters too
Nilotinib has no piperazine ring. That gives a fast way to distinguish it from the other three
drugs in this guide. It also has no sulfur atom and no covalently attached chlorine in the parent
structure. [1,2]
The named product Tasigna contains nilotinib as monohydrochloride monohydrate: a salt
form with one water molecule per formula unit in the crystal. The parent diagram deliberately
leaves out the hydrochloride component and crystal water. Other products can differ in formu-
lation; do not assume every product uses the same solid form. [4]
Check yourself: Do the three F labels mean three separate fluorine-containing rings? No—
three F atoms are attached to a single carbon.

4 · Dasatinib
Look for sulfur in the drug itself




Figure 4: Dasatinib, shown as the neutral anhydrous parent.            Sources: PubChem CID
3062316 and ChEBI:49375. [1,2]


Read it in five chunks
  • D · Thiazole: find the five-membered aromatic ring containing one sulfur and one nitro-
    gen. This is dasatinib’s most useful landmark here. Unlike imatinib mesylate’s counterion
    sulfur, this S is part of the parent drug.
  • B · Amide: locate C(=O)–NH between the thiazole-containing part and a benzene ring.
  • A · Pyrimidine: locate the six-membered aromatic ring with two nitrogens. The extra
    N atoms outside the ring are not counted when naming the ring.
  • C · Piperazine and its hydroxyethyl branch: the non-aromatic two-N ring carries an
    –CH₂–CH₂–OH branch. The final OH is an alcohol; it is not the carbonyl oxygen of the
    amide.
  • Chlorinated benzene: find the Cl directly bonded to a carbon of the benzene ring. A
    methyl branch is also attached to this ring. This chlorine belongs to the drug, not a chloride
    counterion. [1,2]
Memory cue: “Dasatinib = S-ring + one Cl + an OH-ended side chain.”

Keep similar-looking names apart
Thiazole has five ring atoms, including N and S. Pyrimidine has six ring atoms, including two
Ns. Piperazine also has six ring atoms and two Ns, but it is non-aromatic. Ring size and bond
pattern help you tell them apart without memorizing the whole molecule.
Dasatinib can be encountered as a monohydrate; the ChEBI record distinguishes anhydrous
dasatinib from its hydrated form. This page shows only the anhydrous parent, not crystal water.
Water of crystallization is not a new OH branch bonded to the drug. [2,5]
Check yourself: Can you find both oxygen atoms? One is in the amide carbonyl; the other
is in the terminal alcohol. [1,2]

5 · Bosutinib
Find the fused core and the triple bond




Figure 5: Bosutinib, shown as the neutral anhydrous parent.          Sources: PubChem CID
5328940 and ChEBI:39112. [1,2]


Read it in five chunks
  • A · Quinoline core: two aromatic six-membered rings share an edge—two atoms and
    the bond between them. Together, one benzene-like ring and one N-containing ring
    make quinoline. This is a fused system, unlike imatinib’s linked pyridine and pyrimidine.
  • B · Nitrile: find –C≡N extending from the core. The three lines indicate a triple bond. The
    nitrogen at the end is not part of a ring.
  • C · Piperazine tail: the two-N non-aromatic ring connects to the core through –O–CH₂–
    CH₂–CH₂–. Count three carbon atoms between the ether oxygen and the ring nitrogen.
  • Two methoxy groups: each is –O–CH₃. One attaches to the quinoline region and one to
    the separate benzene ring. Together with the side-chain ether, these account for its three
    oxygen atoms.
  • Two chlorines: both Cl atoms are covalently attached to the separate benzene ring. An
    NH links that ring to the quinoline core. [1,2]
Memory cue: “Bosutinib = fused core + nitrile + two Cls.”

The important contrast: no amide
Bosutinib has no C(=O)–N amide group. Its oxygen atoms are in ethers. The NH bridge
between aromatic systems is an amine linkage, not an amide, because there is no adjacent
carbonyl. This distinction is more informative than simply counting Ns and Os. [1,2]
The named product Bosulif contains bosutinib monohydrate. Its crystal water is omitted from
this anhydrous parent drawing. A hydrate contains associated water; that does not mean water
is covalently added to the quinoline skeleton. [6]
Check yourself: Does the N at the end of the triple bond increase the number of nitrogen
atoms inside quinoline? No—it is outside the ring.

Fast identification: Three Fs? Think nilotinib. A five-membered S ring? Dasatinib. A fused
quinoline with C≡N? Bosutinib. A pyridine–pyrimidine pair, amide and piperazine with no halo-
gens? Imatinib. These cues distinguish these four structures, not every drug ever made.
How do different structures block BCR–ABL?
BCR–ABL is a fusion protein with a kinase “engine.” A kinase transfers phosphate from
ATP to a target molecule. In BCR–ABL, abnormally persistent kinase activity can drive leukemia-
associated signaling. These four drugs bind in the ATP-site region and inhibit that activity. [7]
A useful simplified sequence is:
Drug occupies the ATP-site region → kinase activity is reduced → downstream growth
signaling is reduced. [7]
The different rings and substituents place atoms in different positions in three dimensions. Ni-
trogen and oxygen atoms can participate in hydrogen bonding when their bonding state and
environment allow it; carbon-rich aromatic surfaces can make other contacts. Some nitrogens
cannot accept a hydrogen bond—for example, an amide nitrogen is generally not an acceptor.
Actual binding depends on the whole ligand, the protein’s conformation, water and protonation.
[7]

What a drawing cannot tell you
You cannot rank clinical effectiveness, safety, mutation coverage, absorption or solu-
bility by counting rings, fluorines or nitrogens. Nor does a colored region prove that it contacts
a particular protein residue. These require structural, biochemical and clinical evidence. BCR–
ABL inhibitors can differ in their other protein targets as well. This is a structure-learning guide,
not a treatment-selection tool. [3–7]

7 · Make it stick
Mini-glossary

Word                                             Plain-language meaning
Parent structure                                 The drug molecule considered separately
                                                 from counterions or crystal water. Here,
                                                 neutral parents are drawn.
Heteroatom                                       An atom other than carbon or hydrogen; here,
                                                 examples include N, O and S.
Aromatic ring                                    A cyclic system with delocalized electrons; not
                                                 simply “any hexagon.”
Amide                                            A carbonyl directly attached to nitrogen:
                                                 C(=O)–N.
Ether / alcohol                                  Ether: C–O–C. Alcohol: C–OH, where the
                                                 carbon is not a carbonyl carbon.
Counterion / hydrate                             An oppositely charged ion in a salt / a solid
                                                 form containing water.


Self-check: cover the answers first
  1. At an unlabeled corner of a skeletal drawing, which element is implied?
  2. Which of these four parent drugs has no piperazine ring? What two landmarks help identify
     it?
  3. Which parent drug contains sulfur? Why does “imatinib mesylate” not contradict that an-
     swer?
  4. What distinguishes an amide from an ether? Which drug here has a nitrile but no amide?
  5. How do fused rings differ from rings joined by a single bond?
  6. Can you infer the best medicine for a patient from this table? Why or why not?
Answers
1. Carbon; carbon-bound hydrogens are usually omitted. 2. Nilotinib: CF₃ and an imidazole
ring. 3. Dasatinib; imatinib mesylate’s sulfur is in the separate mesylate counterion. 4. Amide
= C(=O)–N; ether = C–O–C. Bosutinib has a nitrile and no amide. 5. The fused rings here share
two adjacent atoms and a bond; singly linked rings do not share ring atoms. 6. No. Patient
factors, disease characteristics, efficacy and safety evidence are not contained in a 2D drawing.
[1–7]

Recommendations · A five-minute study routine
Minute 1: Name the atom letters. Minute 2: Circle the rings and count ring Ns. Minute 3:
Locate amide or nitrile. Minute 4: Identify the signature group and distinguish parent from
salt/hydrate. Minute 5: Close the guide and explain each drug using its memory cue. Repeat
later from memory rather than only rereading.
